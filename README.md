## <img src="https://media.giphy.com/media/iY8CRBdQXODJSCERIr/giphy.gif" width="30"> The Unfiltered Version

> I'm an AI Engineer operating at the intersection of **LLM/GenAI engineering**, **MLOps**, and **production systems architecture** — with 6+ years of experience building AI that ships and stays shipped.

I didn't follow the standard path. I started with a **BTech in Civil Engineering**, spent years preparing for the Indian civil services (UPSC), failed repeatedly, and walked away from everything I'd planned for. That rebuilt me from scratch — and it's why I think about AI systems differently than most engineers.

Civil engineering taught me that **structures carry load or they fail**. UPSC failure taught me that **preparation and execution are different skills**. Data science taught me that **data lies until you question it**. Production AI taught me that **benchmarks don't matter — reliability does**.

I've now spent 6+ years at the intersection of ambitious AI research and the brutal reality of enterprise production systems — at **Microsoft**, **AT&T**, **Infosys**, and **TCS**. My work lives in systems serving millions of real users, not Jupyter notebooks.

<details>
<summary><b>📐 Engineering Principles — How I Think About AI Systems</b></summary>
<br>

### The 10 Things I've Actually Learned

These aren't frameworks I read. They're lessons I got from shipping things that broke in production.

**1. Memory is the moat.**  
Embeddings and retrieval are commodities. How you store, retrieve, and update long-term context — episodic, semantic, procedural — is where differentiation lives.

**2. Reliability is the product.**  
A RAG system that returns accurate answers 90% of the time is not a 90% good product. It's a liability. Production AI requires obsessive reliability engineering.

**3. Governance isn't a checkbox.**  
Every enterprise AI project I've seen fail, failed at governance — not model quality. Build safety, auditability, and alignment in from day one, not sprint 14.

**4. Benchmarks are marketing.**  
MMLU, HumanEval, HELM — they measure models. They don't measure your system. The only benchmark that matters is: does it work for your users, reliably, over time?

**5. Context windows are not memory.**  
Stuffing more tokens into a prompt is not a memory architecture. It's duct tape. Real memory needs structure, retrieval strategy, decay, and consolidation.

**6. Most agent failures are orchestration failures.**  
The individual tool calls usually work. The system fails because the orchestration layer — planning, error recovery, state management — wasn't designed for the real world.

**7. The boring stack wins.**  
FastAPI beats Flask. PostgreSQL beats the cool new database. Postgres with pgvector beats the trendy vector store. Boring infra lets you focus on the hard AI problems.

**8. Every LLM is a junior engineer.**  
You have to write clear specs (prompts), review their work (evals), give them good tools (functions), and build systems that catch their mistakes (guardrails).

**9. Latency is a UX feature, not an infra concern.**  
P50 doesn't matter. P95 doesn't matter. What matters is: when a user needs the system and it's slow, does trust survive? Usually not.

**10. Build for the system that fails, not the demo that works.**  
The demo works in ideal conditions. Production is never ideal. Build for partial failures, hallucinations, timeouts, missing data, and user behavior no one predicted.

</details>

<summary><b>🌟 What Drives Me — The Why Behind the Work</b></summary>

### Why I Build What I Build

I'm not chasing the latest model release or paper. I'm obsessed with a specific problem:

**Why do AI systems forget?**

Every time you start a new conversation with an LLM, it starts fresh. No memory of who you are. No memory of what worked before. No memory of what it has already tried. This is a fundamental architectural limitation, and it's the core of what I'm working to solve.

Humans don't work this way. A good doctor remembers your history. A great mentor remembers how you think. The best systems — biological and engineered — accumulate and apply experience over time.

I believe the next era of AI isn't about bigger context windows or better benchmarks. It's about **memory architecture** — how AI systems store, retrieve, update, and apply long-term knowledge across interactions, sessions, and tasks.

That's what gets me out of bed. Not the hype. The unsolved problem.

**And outside of work?**

I read obsessively across disciplines — kernel design, distributed systems, cognitive science, philosophy of mind. I believe the best AI engineers are secretly systems thinkers who happened to find AI. The field rewards depth and curiosity above all.

I'm also deeply interested in what happens when you build AI for people who aren't technical. The gap between what AI can do and what most people can access is enormous. Closing that gap — through better interfaces, better memory, better reliability — is a mission worth dedicating a career to.
